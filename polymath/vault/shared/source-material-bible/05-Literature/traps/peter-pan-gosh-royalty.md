# Trap Dossier — Peter Pan & the GOSH Perpetual Royalty

> The only work in Western literature with a copyright that, in one country, never ends.

## The two jurisdictions diverge sharply

**United States.** J. M. Barrie's stage play *Peter Pan, or The Boy Who Wouldn't Grow Up* (1904) and the novel *Peter and Wendy* (1911) are both well clear of the 1930 cutoff and are firmly in the US public domain. The original text, the characters, and the original illustrations are free to use in the US.

**United Kingdom and EU.** Barrie died in 1937, so ordinary copyright expired at the end of 2007 (life + 70). But Britain did something unique. In 1929 Barrie gifted the rights to Great Ormond Street Hospital (GOSH), the London children's hospital. When the work was heading toward public domain, **Section 301 / Schedule 6 of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988** granted GOSH a **perpetual right to a royalty** on commercial performances, publications, and adaptations of *Peter Pan* in the UK — a right that continues *forever*, even though the underlying copyright has expired.

## What this means in practice

- A US-based content operation publishing US-facing Peter Pan products built on Barrie's original text is on safe public-domain ground.
- A UK-facing commercial product — a stage performance, a published edition, a major adaptation sold in the UK — may owe a royalty to GOSH under the perpetual right, regardless of copyright status. The right is narrow (it is a royalty right, not a veto) but it is real and it is permanent.
- The amount and mechanism are administered by GOSH; for anything substantial sold into the UK market, contact them.

## The second trap: the Disney design

Quite separately, Disney's animated *Peter Pan* (1953) created the now-iconic look: the bright-green tunic and cap with a red feather, the specific Tinker Bell design, the particular Captain Hook styling. **That design is a live Disney copyright and trademark.** Barrie's text being public domain does not free Disney's drawings. See [[traps/winnie-the-pooh-vs-disney]] for the identical pattern.

## What is safe to use
- The story, characters, and names (Peter Pan, Wendy, Tinker Bell, Captain Hook, the Lost Boys, Neverland, the crocodile, Smee).
- Barrie's original prose and the famous lines ("To die will be an awfully big adventure"; "second star to the right and straight on till morning").
- Original early-edition illustrations (e.g. F. D. Bedford's 1911 illustrations) — public domain.
- The Neverland *aesthetic* rendered in your own design language.

## What to avoid / handle with care
- For **UK commercial use**: budget for, or clear, the GOSH royalty.
- Disney's 1953 green-outfit Peter Pan, Disney's Tinker Bell mascot (a live Disney trademark), Disney's Hook.
- Implying any GOSH or Disney endorsement.

## The takeaway for the pipeline
Mark Peter Pan `caution`, not `safe`, because of the GOSH perpetual right in the UK and the Disney design overlay. The safe path: original Barrie text + original-edition imagery, US-market focus, your own visual design. For UK-facing scale, clear GOSH.
