# Trap Dossier — Winnie-the-Pooh: 1926 Book vs. Disney Version

> The honey bear is a perfect teaching case: the book is free, the brand is not.

## What is now free
- **A. A. Milne's *Winnie-the-Pooh* (1926)** entered the US public domain on **1 January 2022** (95-year term expired).
- **E. H. Shepard's original line illustrations** from that 1926 book entered the US public domain at the same time — the classic, sparse, hand-drawn Pooh.
- ***The House at Pooh Corner* (1928)** entered the US public domain on **1 January 2024**, which is significant because **Tigger first appears in that 1928 book.** So the *literary* Tigger is now free; the Shepard-drawn Tigger is free.
- The characters as Milne wrote them: Pooh, Piglet, Eeyore, Owl, Kanga, Roo, Rabbit, Christopher Robin, and the Hundred Acre Wood.

The 2023 indie horror film *Winnie-the-Pooh: Blood and Honey* was made specifically because the 1926 material went public domain — a public demonstration that the original is usable.

## What is still locked
- **Disney's Pooh design.** Disney licensed Pooh in 1961 and redesigned him: the bright **red crop-top shirt**, rounder body, specific facial proportions. That redesign is a separate copyright owned by Disney and **does not expire when Milne's book does.**
- **The Disney character designs** of Tigger, Piglet, Eeyore (the specific Disney looks), and Disney's Tigger in particular.
- **"Winnie the Pooh" as a Disney trademark.** Disney holds registered trademarks on Pooh branding for merchandise. A trademark can last forever as long as it is used in commerce. This is the part most people miss: even with the book in the public domain, you cannot brand merchandise in a way that suggests it is Disney's Pooh, or that uses the Disney trademark.

## The clean rule
**Copy Shepard, never Disney.** If your Pooh is the spare, line-drawn 1926 Shepard bear (often shirtless, or in the original muted style), drawn in your own hand, you are working from public-domain material. If your Pooh has the red shirt and the Disney proportions, you are infringing a live copyright and likely a trademark.

Avoid using "Winnie the Pooh" as a *product brand name* in a way that competes with or imitates Disney's trademarked use — the safer path for owned branding is an original Z-named bear character of your own.

## What is safe
- The 1926 and 1928 Milne text and **quotes verified against the original books**.
- Your own illustration in the spirit of Shepard's public-domain originals.
- The Hundred Acre Wood setting and pastoral, cozy aesthetic.

> **MISATTRIBUTION TRAP (added in verification 2026-05-31):** The two most-shared "Pooh" quotes online — *"Sometimes the smallest things take up the most room in your heart"* and *"How lucky I am to have something that makes saying goodbye so hard"* — do **NOT** appear in Milne's 1926/1928 books. They are widely flagged as later inventions, most likely from **Disney adaptations** (the second is attributed to Disney's *Pooh's Grand Adventure*). Putting these on merch as "Milne / public-domain Pooh" is exactly the Disney-layer trap this dossier warns against — it pairs Disney-origin text with the Pooh name. **Do not use either quote as public-domain Milne material.** Only quote lines you have confirmed verbatim in the 1926/1928 text (e.g. via Wikisource / Project Gutenberg Canada).

## What to avoid
- Disney's red-shirt Pooh and Disney's character designs.
- The Disney "Winnie the Pooh" trademark on merch branding.
- Reproducing Shepard's *exact* drawings as a brand logo (copying the public-domain art is fine; the issue is only the Disney layer).

## The takeaway for the pipeline
Pooh is `caution`, not `safe`, purely because of the Disney design-and-trademark overlay. The instruction to a generator is blunt: **"Pooh, in the original 1926 Shepard line-art style, never the Disney red-shirt version."**
