# Caduceus

> The winged, twin-serpent staff of Hermes — the true emblem of commerce and negotiation, and the most famously *misused* symbol in the modern world.

## Who / What
The caduceus is the herald's wand carried by Hermes (Roman Mercury): a short staff topped with wings and entwined by two serpents in a double-helix pattern. As Hermes's attribute, it properly symbolizes commerce, negotiation, messengers, travel, and trade. It is one of the most graphically appealing ancient symbols, but it is also the subject of a persistent and important error: it is frequently mistaken for the symbol of medicine. The genuine medical symbol is the **Rod of Asclepius** — a single snake around a plain staff, with no wings. This entry exists partly to flag that distinction.

## Archetype
The Emblem of Trade / Symbol of Negotiation / Herald's Mark. The badge of the go-between, the diplomat, and the merchant.

## Visual Motifs (safe to draw)
A short staff with a pair of wings spread at the top and two snakes coiled symmetrically around it in a double helix; usually rendered in gold or bronze; clean, heraldic, and balanced.

## Symbols
- **Twin entwined serpents** — balance, duality, negotiation.
- **Wings** — Hermes's speed; the messenger.
- **The staff** — authority and heraldry.

## Themes
Commerce and trade, negotiation and diplomacy, communication and messengers, balance and duality, travel — and, as a cautionary theme, the danger of a misunderstood symbol.

Its origin as Hermes's wand. The legend that Hermes used it to separate two fighting snakes, which then coiled around it in peace (negotiation embodied). The historical accident by which it became confused with the medical Rod of Asclepius in the US.

## Legal & Safety
- **Free to use:** the caduceus symbol (ancient, public domain).
- **Caution:** do NOT use the caduceus to imply medical/healthcare authority — that is the Rod of Asclepius (one snake, no wings). Misusing it for medical branding is misleading. Specific government/military caduceus insignia are restricted.
