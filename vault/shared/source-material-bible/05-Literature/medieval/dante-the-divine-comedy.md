# The Divine Comedy

> The definitive imaginative map of the afterlife — nine circles of Hell, a mountain of Purgatory, and the rose of Paradise.

## Public-domain status
Dante's *Divine Comedy* (*Inferno*, *Purgatorio*, *Paradiso*) was completed around 1320; the original Italian is permanently free. Use a public-domain translation — Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's, Henry Francis Cary's, or Charles Eliot Norton's — or your own. Avoid recent in-copyright translations (Allen Mandelbaum, Robert/Jean Hollander, Mary Jo Bang). **Crucially, Gustave Doré's celebrated 1860s engravings of the Comedy are public domain** and are the gold-standard visual reference. See [[traps/translations-and-editions]] and Section 06.

## What it is
The poet Dante, lost in a dark wood at midlife, is guided by the Roman poet Virgil down through the nine concentric circles of Hell (*Inferno*) — each punishing a category of sin with a fitting torment — then up the terraced Mountain of Purgatory (*Purgatorio*), and finally, guided by his idealized beloved Beatrice, through the celestial spheres to the vision of God (*Paradiso*). It is at once a personal allegory, a political broadside, and the most detailed imaginative architecture of the Christian afterlife ever written.

## Themes & symbols
- **Themes:** sin and its precise consequences; redemption and the soul's ascent; divine justice; love as the force that "moves the sun and the other stars"; the midlife crisis of the soul.
- **Symbols:** the nine circles of Hell, the dark wood, the three beasts (leopard, lion, she-wolf), the gate of Hell ("Abandon all hope, ye who enter here"), the mountain of Purgatory, the celestial rose.
- **Archetypes:** the pilgrim, the wise guide (Virgil), the beloved intercessor (Beatrice), the damned.

## Visual & emotional palette
Descending rings of an infernal funnel; frozen and burning landscapes; a terraced mountain climbing toward light; a blinding empyrean rose. Awe and dread shading into transcendence. Palette: hellfire orange-black, purgatorial dawn-grey, paradisal gold-white. Doré's high-contrast black-and-white engravings define the look.

## The trap (if any)
No trap in the source; translation trap only. Doré's engravings are free; Botticelli's and Blake's Dante illustrations are also public domain.

## Applications / remix hooks
