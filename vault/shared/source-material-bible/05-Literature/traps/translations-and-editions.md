# Trap Dossier — Translations, Critical Editions & Annotations

> The original is 3,000 years old. The translation you are quoting is from 2018. Only one of those is free.

## The core rule

Copyright protects *creative expression*. A translator makes thousands of creative word-choice decisions; an editor writes original annotations and introductions; a designer creates an original typeset. Each of those is **a new, independently copyrightable work layered on top of the public-domain original.** The age of the underlying text does not free the new layer.

This is the most common quiet mistake in working with old literature: people assume "Homer is ancient, so any Homer is free." Wrong — *Homer's Greek* is free; a *recent English translation* is a fresh copyright owned by its translator or publisher.

## The traps, by type

### 1. Modern translations
A recent translation is a brand-new copyrighted work.
- Emily Wilson's *Odyssey* (2018) and *Iliad* (2023) — copyrighted.
- Seamus Heaney's *Beowulf* (1999) — copyrighted.
- Robert Fagles's Homer (1990s) — copyrighted.
- Pevear & Volokhonsky's Tolstoy and Dostoevsky translations — copyrighted.
- **Safe alternatives:** older public-domain translations — e.g. Samuel Butler's or Alexander Pope's Homer, Constance Garnett's Tolstoy/Dostoevsky, the older Beowulf translations (Gummere, Morris). These are pre-cutoff and free.

### 2. Critical / scholarly editions
The *text* of a public-domain novel is free, but a modern critical edition adds copyrighted apparatus: a new introduction, explanatory footnotes, textual variant notes, a glossary, an editor's chosen "definitive" reconstruction of the text. Norton Critical Editions, Oxford World's Classics introductions, Penguin Classics notes — the **added material is copyrighted**, even though the core novel is not. Use the bare public-domain text (e.g. from Project Gutenberg, Wikisource, Standard Ebooks), not the scholarly apparatus.

### 3. Annotations and modern introductions
A famous author's modern foreword to a public-domain classic, or a scholar's annotations, are their own copyrighted works. The classic is free; the foreword is not.

### 4. Specific font / typeset files
A particular digital font file and a particular publisher's typesetting can carry their own rights (font *software* is protected; typeface designs vary by jurisdiction). Set public-domain text in an openly-licensed or your own font; do not lift a publisher's exact typeset page as an asset.

### 5. Specific cover artwork and 20th-century illustrations
A 1950 cover illustration of a public-domain novel is a separate copyrighted artwork. Use original-edition (pre-cutoff) illustrations or your own art. See [[01-Legal-Guidelines/pd-source-vs-modern-adaptation]].

## The clean workflow for Polymath
1. Source the **original-language or pre-cutoff public-domain text/translation** (Project Gutenberg, Wikisource, Standard Ebooks, HathiTrust public-domain scans).
2. If you need a modern-feeling translation, **commission or produce your own** (including AI-assisted translation you then own/clear), rather than copying an in-copyright one.
3. Use **original-edition illustrations** (pre-cutoff) or your own art.
4. Set text in your **own or an openly-licensed font**.
5. Never reproduce a modern edition's introduction, notes, or apparatus.

## The takeaway for the pipeline
When an entry says "use an old public-domain translation," this dossier is why. Treat any *specific modern translation/edition/annotation* as `caution`/`restricted` even when the source work is `public-domain`.
