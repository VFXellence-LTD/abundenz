# Trap Dossier — Tarzan & John Carter: PD Text, Live Trademarks

> The classic "copyright expired but trademark never did" case — and the owner enforces hard.

## The split status

Edgar Rice Burroughs published *Tarzan of the Apes* and *A Princess of Mars* (the John Carter of Mars story) in 1912. Both are well before the 1930 cutoff, so the **original novels' text is in the US public domain**. Several early sequels are also public domain; later ones (1930s onward) may still be under copyright on a book-by-book basis, so check each title against the moving cutoff.

That means you can legally:
- Reproduce the original 1912 text.
- Retell the stories.
- Use the public-domain plot, world, and original characters as written.

## The trap: live registered trademarks

**Edgar Rice Burroughs, Inc. (ERB Inc.)** — the company the author set up — owns **live US registered trademarks** in "TARZAN" and "JOHN CARTER" (and related marks like "JOHN CARTER OF MARS", "EDGAR RICE BURROUGHS"). Trademarks do not expire with copyright; they last as long as they are used and defended, and ERB Inc. is well known for actively policing them.

So the names function as brands. Using "Tarzan" or "John Carter" as:
- a **product or merchandise brand**,
- a **title** that implies an official or sponsored source,
- a **logo or mascot**,

risks a trademark claim *even though the underlying story is public domain*. Trademark law protects against consumer confusion about source, not against retelling a story.

## The clean rule
**Tell the story, drop the brand name.** The public-domain freedom is in the *narrative and text*; the restriction is in the *name as a commercial identifier*. The safe move for a Polymath product is to:
- adapt the public-domain plot, themes, and world (feral man raised by apes; Earthman transported to a dying Mars), and
- give your hero an original Z-name of your own, with original character design and branding.

This is exactly how many "sword and planet" and "jungle hero" works operate downstream of Burroughs.

## What is safe
- The 1912 novels' text and the public-domain story beats.
- The genre templates Burroughs invented: the jungle-raised noble hero; the "sword and planet" / planetary-romance adventure on a dying Mars (Barsoom).
- Pulp-era cover-art *aesthetic* (your own art).

## What to avoid
- "Tarzan" or "John Carter" as a brand name, title, mascot, or merch identifier.
- Disney's 1999 animated Tarzan design and the 2012 *John Carter* film designs (separate live film copyrights).
- Burroughs's later, still-copyrighted sequels.

## The takeaway for the pipeline
Mark both `caution`. The generation instruction: "Use the public-domain story and world; never use the names 'Tarzan' or 'John Carter' as a brand; create an original Z-named hero instead."
