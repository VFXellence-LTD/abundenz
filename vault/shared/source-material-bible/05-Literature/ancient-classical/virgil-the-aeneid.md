# The Aeneid

> Rome's national epic — the survivor of Troy who founds a new empire at the cost of everything he loves.

## Public-domain status
Virgil's *Aeneid* was written 29–19 BCE; the original Latin is permanently free. Use a public-domain translation — John Dryden's grand verse, John Conington, or Theodore Williams — or your own. Avoid recent in-copyright translations (Robert Fagles, Sarah Ruden, Shadi Bartsch). See [[traps/translations-and-editions]].

## What it is
After Troy falls, the Trojan prince Aeneas leads a band of survivors on a long sea voyage to Italy, where fate has decreed they will found the line that becomes Rome. Along the way he has a doomed love affair with Dido, Queen of Carthage, who kills herself when he leaves to follow his destiny; he descends into the underworld to see his father and a vision of Rome's future; and he fights a final war in Italy, killing his rival Turnus in the poem's abrupt, troubling final line. It is the Roman answer to Homer — the first half echoes the *Odyssey* (the wandering), the second half the *Iliad* (the war).

## Themes & symbols
- **Themes:** duty (*pietas*) over personal desire; destiny and the cost of founding; exile and homelessness; love sacrificed to fate; the price of empire.
- **Symbols:** burning Troy, the golden bough (passport to the underworld), the shield of Aeneas (depicting future Rome), Dido's funeral pyre.
- **Archetypes:** the reluctant, duty-bound hero; the abandoned queen; the founder; the pious man.

## Visual & emotional palette
The night Troy burns; a fleet of exiles on a dark sea; a descent into the shades; the building of a new city. Solemn, melancholic, monumental. Palette: fire-orange against night, sea-grey, underworld-violet, imperial gold and purple.

## The trap (if any)
No trap in the source; translation trap only. Renaissance and neoclassical paintings of Dido and Aeneas are largely public domain (Section 06) and make strong reference.
