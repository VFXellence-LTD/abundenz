# Oedipus Rex (Oedipus the King)

> The defining tragedy of fate — a man who solves every riddle except the one about himself.

## Public-domain status
Sophocles' *Oedipus Rex* was first performed around 429 BCE; the original Greek is permanently free. Use a public-domain translation — Sir Richard Jebb, F. Storr (Loeb), or E. H. Plumptre — or your own. Avoid recent in-copyright translations (e.g. those by Robert Fagles, David Grene). See [[traps/translations-and-editions]].

## What it is
Thebes is dying of plague. King Oedipus vows to find the murderer of the previous king, Laius, whose death has cursed the city. As he investigates, the horrifying truth emerges: Oedipus himself unknowingly killed Laius — his own father — at a crossroads, and then married Laius's widow, Jocasta — his own mother. He had been abandoned as an infant precisely to avoid the prophecy that he would do these things, and the very steps taken to prevent it caused it. Jocasta hangs herself; Oedipus blinds himself with her brooches and goes into exile. The play that, more than any other, defines dramatic irony and the tragedy of fate.

## Themes & symbols
- **Themes:** fate versus free will; the limits of knowledge and the danger of seeking truth; hubris; the gap between sight and insight (the seeing king is blind to himself; the blind prophet sees all).
- **Symbols:** the Sphinx and her riddle ("what walks on four legs, then two, then three?"), the crossroads, blinded eyes, the swollen foot that names him, the plague.
- **Archetypes:** the tragic king, the blind prophet (Tiresias), the relentless seeker of truth.

## Visual & emotional palette
Ancient Thebes, the tragic mask, a crossroads under a hard sky, the Sphinx crouched at a city gate, the moment of self-blinding. Inexorable, dreadful, cathartic. Palette: dust and stone, blood-red, shadowed eyes, Greek-theatre ochre.

## The trap (if any)
No trap in the source; translation trap only. The "Oedipus complex" is Freud's 20th-century framing — you can reference and discuss the concept freely, but specific Freud texts and modern psychoanalytic editions have their own status.
