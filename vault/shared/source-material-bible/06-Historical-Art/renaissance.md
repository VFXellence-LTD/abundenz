# Renaissance Art

> The rebirth of classical ideals through new science: linear perspective, idealized humanist figures, soft sfumato light, and balanced, harmonious composition.

## Overview
The Renaissance fused revived classical antiquity with new techniques — linear perspective, anatomical study, oil glazing, sfumato — to produce the West's canonical "old master" look. Its balanced compositions, luminous skin, and dignified humanism are public domain and immensely remixable, including the popular "classical painting meets modern object" meme format.

## Defining Features
- Linear (one-point) perspective creating convincing depth.
- Idealized but anatomically studied human figures.
- Balanced, often triangular or symmetrical compositions.
- Sfumato — soft, smoky transitions with no hard edges (Leonardo).
- Classical architecture and allegory as setting and subject.

## Visual Vocabulary
Luminous oil-glazed skin, deep receding space, calm dignified faces, rich draped fabric, putti (cherubs), classical columns and arches, allegorical figures, the three-quarter portrait.

## Shapes
The stable triangle/pyramid composition, the perspective grid and vanishing point, the rounded arch, the halo, the gentle S-curve of the figure, the oval portrait format.

## Textures
Smooth oil glaze, sfumato haze, fresco plaster, gilded frame, velvet and brocade fabric, marble.

## Palette
The "old master" family: warm umbers and ochres, deep crimson, ultramarine blue, gold, ivory flesh tones, and shadowy chiaroscuro browns. Rich and luminous. See [[08-Color-Palettes/README]].

## Key Motifs
The Madonna-and-child triangle, the putto/cherub, the idealized male nude, the perspective interior, allegory and personification, the dignified profile/three-quarter portrait, classical ruins and columns.

## Rights Note
The STYLE and the original masterworks are public domain — the artists died centuries ago. The key nuance is **photographic reproductions**: in the US a faithful photo of a flat public-domain painting is itself public domain (*Bridgeman v. Corel*), but some institutions assert terms and the rule varies by country. For direct image use, prefer confirmed open-access museum scans (e.g. Wikimedia, Met Open Access); for generation, prompt the *style* freely.
