# Ancient Egyptian Art

> Three thousand years of rigorously ordered sacred imagery: figures in profile with frontal eyes, gods and kings rendered to a fixed canon, banded with hieroglyphs and gold.

## Overview
Egyptian art is one of the most stable visual systems ever devised — a canon held with little change for three millennia because its purpose was eternity, not novelty. Its strict frontality, register bands, hierarchical scale, and symbol-dense surfaces make it instantly readable and endlessly remixable. All authentic ancient works are public domain.

## Defining Features
- The composite "profile" convention: head, legs, and feet in profile; eye and torso frontal.
- Hierarchical scale — the more important the figure (pharaoh, god), the larger.
- Registers: scenes stacked in horizontal bands like comic panels.
- Symbolic, flat color rather than naturalistic shading.
- Hieroglyphs integrated as both text and decoration.

## Visual Vocabulary
Crisp outlines filled with flat, symbolic color; rigid grids and baselines; gods identified by attribute (Anubis' jackal head, Horus' falcon head); processions reading left to right or toward a focal deity. Gold leaf and lapis blue signal divinity and royalty.

## Shapes
Strong horizontals and verticals, the triangular silhouette of the seated/striding figure, the lotus and papyrus column capital, the rounded scarab, the trapezoidal pylon, the circular sun-disc.

## Textures
Flat painted plaster, carved relief edges, polished gold, lapis and turquoise inlay, papyrus weave, sandstone.

## Palette
The "gold and lapis" royal family: gold, lapis blue, turquoise, carnelian red, black, white, and ochre. Rich, saturated, regal. See [[08-Color-Palettes/README]].

## Key Motifs
Ankh, Eye of Horus (wedjat), scarab beetle, winged sun-disc, lotus and papyrus, falcon (Horus), jackal (Anubis), cartouche frame, was-scepter, djed-pillar, the seated/striding figure.

## Rights Note
The STYLE and all authentic ancient works are public domain. Two cautions: a **modern photograph** of a museum artifact may carry the photographer's/museum's copyright (recreate the imagery rather than lifting a specific photo), and a **modern studio's "Egyptian" fantasy design** (a specific mummy, a specific film god) is that studio's own copyright — emulate the authentic ancient canon, not a Hollywood version.
