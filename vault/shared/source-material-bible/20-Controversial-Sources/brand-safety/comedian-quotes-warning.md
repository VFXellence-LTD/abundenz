# Comedian Quotes Warning — DO NOT USE

> Modern comedian quotes are almost never safe for commercial products. Three independent legal regimes overlap to protect them. The safe lane is narrow and well-defined. This file maps both.

---

## Why comedian quotes are triple-protected

Unlike ancient myths or historical documents, a comedian's material sits at the intersection of three distinct and independently-enforced legal regimes:

### 1. Copyright
Jokes, comic bits, stand-up material, and comedic observations are **copyrightable creative expression**. A one-liner is not protected merely because it is short — brevity does not eliminate copyright (a haiku is copyrighted; a limerick is copyrighted). The creative choices in a joke — the setup, the punchline wording, the specific comic construction — are expression, and expression is what copyright protects.

This covers:
- Stand-up comedy bits (written and performed)
- Quoted lines from comedy films and TV shows (the writer holds copyright in the script)
- Published books of humor writing
- Podcast and interview one-liners (potentially protected in their original expression)

**Consequence:** Printing a modern comedian's joke or well-known line on a t-shirt without a license is copyright infringement. The fact that the line is widely circulated on the internet does not make it free.

### 2. Trademark
Comedian catchphrases are frequently registered as trademarks when they have commercial value as a brand identifier. Examples of known trademark situations:

- **"Git-R-Done"** — registered trademark of Daniel Lawrence Whitney (Larry the Cable Guy). Class 25 registration. Do not use on products.
- Various other performers' signature catchphrases have been trademarked in Class 25 and related classes.

**Consequence:** Using a trademarked catchphrase on a commercial product is trademark infringement regardless of whether the expression is also copyrighted. The two claims are independent.

### 3. Right of publicity
A living comedian's name, image, and persona are protected by right-of-publicity law. Using a comedian's name on a product ("A Jim Gaffigan-inspired mug") or depicting their likeness without permission commercially exploits their identity.

**Consequence:** Even if a specific joke or catchphrase were somehow not copyright-protected or trademarked, using the comedian's name to market a product based on their persona is a right-of-publicity violation.

## The pattern that looks safe but isn't

A common mistake: "This quote is everywhere on Pinterest — it must be free."

Widespread internet circulation of a quote does not affect its copyright status. Viral social media sharing does not create a "deemed public domain" result. Every comedian's line being shared as an inspirational graphic on Instagram is still under that comedian's (or their employer's/network's) copyright. Using those lines on commercial products exposes the seller to infringement claims — and the platforms where you sell.

## The narrow safe lane

The only safely usable comedian and wit quotations for commercial products are those from **authors whose works are fully public domain** — meaning the work was published 1930 or earlier in the US AND the author died long enough ago that life+70 has expired.

### Confirmed safe (for US commercial use)

**Mark Twain** (d. 1910)
- Works published before his death: all PD. Works published 1930 or earlier: PD.
- One of the richest sources of commercially usable wit in the English language.
- **Misattribution alert:** Many famous "Mark Twain" quotes circulating online are fabricated. Use only quotes verified to a specific published Twain work. See [[12-Quotes/README|12-Quotes]] for the verified Twain quote collection.
- Source works: *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1884), *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* (1876), *Following the Equator* (1897), notebooks and speeches — all PD.

**Oscar Wilde** (d. 1900)
- Works published before 1930: all PD.
- Brilliant, quotable, specifically suited to typographic products.
- Works: *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895), *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1890), aphorisms from interviews and essays — all PD.
- **Misattribution alert:** Many Oscar Wilde quotes online are fabricated or wrongly attributed. Verify against the [[12-Quotes/README|12-Quotes]] verified collection.

**Ambrose Bierce** (d. 1914, presumed)
- Works: *The Devil's Dictionary* (1906 collected edition — PD) and other work published before 1930.
- Sharp satirical wit; niche audience; commercial product potential for literary/dark-humor market.

**Will Rogers** (d. 1935)
- Works published before 1931: PD. Works published 1931 or later: verify per work (95-year rule applies; a 1932 Rogers piece enters PD in 2028).
- Very quotable American humorist; patriotic content crossover potential.
- **Per-work verification needed:** Rogers died in 1935 so his works span both sides of the 1930 PD cutoff. Check publication year of each specific quote before use.

## NOT safe — common mistakes

### Dorothy Parker (d. 1967)
Dorothy Parker is one of the most-quoted wits in POD, and almost all "Dorothy Parker quote" products are infringing.

- Parker died June 7, 1967. Under the life+70 rule, her works enter public domain in 2038 (at the earliest, in jurisdictions that use life+70 from death year).
- Under US copyright rules for works published between 1928 and 1977, the analysis is work-by-work — many of her works published in that window are still in copyright term.
- **Bottom line:** Do not use Dorothy Parker quotes on commercial products. Not in 2026. Not until 2038 at the earliest, and only after per-work analysis.
- Mark `risk_level: avoid` for all Parker quotes.

### Any living comedian
Every living stand-up comedian, comedy writer, TV comedy personality, or late-night host has active copyright, likely active trademark (for catchphrases), and right-of-publicity protection. **Do not use.** This includes: John Mulaney, Nikki Glaser, Dave Chappelle, Hannah Gadsby, Jimmy Fallon/Kimmel/Colbert material, Saturday Night Live cast material, any currently-airing comedy series character quotes.

### Recent TV and film quotes
Lines from *The Office*, *Schitt's Creek*, *Parks and Recreation*, *Arrested Development*, *Seinfeld*, *Friends*, *The Big Bang Theory*, and every other comedy series are copyrighted — the network/studio holds copyright in the scripts. "That's what she said" is Michael Scott (The Office) and is protected. "Yada yada yada" is Seinfeld content. None of these are safe for commercial products.
