# Sacred Texts — Cultural Sensitivity, Depiction Taboos & Respectful Use

Copyright clearance is a legal question. Cultural respect is a business question. This file covers the latter: what does respectful use look like in practice, what are the hard depiction taboos, and what is the difference between thoughtful faith content and lazy appropriation.

---

## The core principle

> **A product that is legally safe can still destroy your brand, generate organized boycotts, and get your storefront removed by platform trust-and-safety teams.**

Faith and sacred content carries meaning that matters deeply to real people. Handling it without care is not just an ethical failure — it is a commercial one. The highest-revenue faith content is produced by operators who understand the tradition well enough to create products that genuine members of that tradition would actually buy and be proud to own.

---

## Hard depiction taboos by tradition

### Islam
**Do not depict the Prophet Muhammad.** This is non-negotiable for commercial products.

- The prohibition on depicting the Prophet is mainstream, not fringe — it applies across Sunni and Shia traditions and represents the considered religious position of the vast majority of the world's 1.8+ billion Muslims.
- This applies to all image types: illustration, photography, AI-generated images, and representational graphic design.
- It applies regardless of how the depiction is framed: reverent, neutral, satirical, or abstract.
- Commercial products with such depictions will be flagged by Muslim buyers on every platform, resulting in takedowns and account-health damage.
- The rule applies to depictions of all Prophets recognized in Islam (including Jesus, Moses, and Abraham, who are Islamic prophets). Exercise caution with any figurative depiction of recognized prophets in an Islamic-market context.
- **The commercial opportunity in Islamic content** is entirely in non-figurative forms: calligraphy, geometry, the 99 names of God, and typographic products using PD translated text. This lane is rich, underserved, and culturally safe.

### Judaism
- Do not depict God in physical/figurative form. The prohibition on graven images (the Second Commandment) is a live religious commitment for observant Jewish consumers, and depicting the divine in anthropomorphic form is broadly considered inappropriate.
- Authentic representation matters: Jewish religious symbols (the Star of David, the menorah) in a commercial context are broadly accepted when used respectfully. Combining these symbols with other traditions' symbols (e.g., a Star of David inside an Om) without real knowledge of both traditions is likely to read as superficial.
- Holocaust imagery: using Holocaust-adjacent imagery (striped uniforms, the Star of David badge, camp imagery) for commercial design purposes — even without malicious intent — is deeply offensive and will generate severe backlash. This is a hard avoid for any decorative or apparel application.

### Hinduism
- **Deity images on footwear, bathroom products, or floor coverings:** Placing images of Hindu deities on products meant to be worn on feet or placed on the floor is considered deeply disrespectful across the Hindu tradition. This has generated major platform complaints and organized campaigns against POD sellers who have done it (including several well-publicized incidents on Amazon and Redbubble involving Ganesha and Lakshmi imagery).
- **Lakshmi, Ganesha, Shiva, and other deities on alcohol-adjacent products or in undignified contexts:** Broadly considered offensive.
- **The rule:** Deity imagery is best avoided for merch applications entirely unless you have deep knowledge of the tradition and are serving an explicitly Hindu audience that would endorse the use.

### Buddhism
- Avoid the manji/Buddhist swastika in any Western commercial context. See [[09-Symbols/_restricted/swastika-warning|swastika-warning]].
- Buddha imagery: Seated Buddha statues and imagery are broadly acceptable in Western mindfulness/wellness market contexts, where this imagery has become culturally normalized as a symbol of peace and contemplation. However, using the Buddha's image on products meant to be treated disrespectfully (e.g., placed on the floor, used in a humorous context that trivializes the figure) will offend Buddhist communities.
- **Foot rule:** As with Hindu deities, Buddha imagery should not appear on floor coverings, doormats, or shoe-adjacent products.

### Christianity
- Generally more commercially permissive than other traditions in a Western commercial context. Faith-niche Christian products (KJV scripture, cross imagery, devotional phrases) are a well-established commercial category with mainstream consumer acceptance.
- Avoid mock-reverent or blasphemous framing. Products that combine Christian symbols with explicit, crude, or mocking content generate platform flags and negative reviews from Christian buyers.
- Different denominations have different visual cultures; content aimed at evangelical Protestant audiences may look different from content aimed at Catholic audiences. Understanding the audience improves conversion.

## The appropriation line

"Appropriation" is distinct from "inspiration" or "respectful tribute." The line:

**Inspiration / tribute (generally acceptable):**
- Creating a typography poster with a verified PD translation of the Dhammapada and clearly attributing it as Buddhist scripture.
- Designing a Psalms print using JPS 1917 text with the citation visible.
- Geometric Islamic pattern art that draws on the mathematical tradition of Islamic geometric design.

**Appropriation (problematic):**
- Using religious symbols as decorative motifs without any substantive context or knowledge — the Om on a yoga mat sold by a company with no connection to any Hindu or Buddhist tradition is "appropriation" in its most commercial form.
- Mixing sacred symbols from different traditions in a jumbled collage that suggests no understanding of any of them.
- Treating sacred text as font decoration, divorced from its religious meaning and context.

The practical test: **would a thoughtful, knowledgeable member of this faith tradition consider this product respectful?** If the honest answer is "probably not," do not produce it.

---

## Platform policy considerations

Most major POD platforms do not explicitly restrict respectful religious content. However:
- Content that is flagged en masse by members of a faith community is removed quickly under "offensive content" policies.
- Sellers with repeated flags face account suspension.
- The cost of a single high-profile controversy (social media pile-on, organized complaint campaign) can far exceed the revenue from the problematic product.

Respectful, well-researched faith content avoids all of these risks and also builds a loyal buyer base who will recommend products within their community.

## Summary guidance for production teams

| Tradition | Safe commercial lane | Hard avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Christianity | KJV scripture typography, cross imagery, devotional phrases | Blasphemous/mock-reverent combinations |
| Islam | Arabic calligraphy, geometric patterns, 99 Names typography, PD Pickthall text | Any depiction of the Prophet; any depiction of Prophets in Islamic context |
| Judaism | Psalms/Proverbs typography (JPS 1917), Hebrew letterforms, Star of David | Holocaust imagery for decoration; deity depictions; mixing traditions superficially |
| Buddhism | Lotus imagery, dharma wheel, Om typography, seated-Buddha imagery (with care), Dhammapada aphorisms | Manji symbol; Buddha on floor products; trivializing context |
| Hinduism | Sanskrit typography, lotus, Om, geometric mandalas | Deity images on foot products, on alcohol-adjacent items, or in undignified contexts |
