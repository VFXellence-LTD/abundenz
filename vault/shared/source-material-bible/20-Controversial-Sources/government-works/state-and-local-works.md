# State & Local Government Works — Rights Status

State constitutions, state statutes, and state court opinions occupy a grayer zone than their federal counterparts. The result is almost always the same — they are public domain — but the legal path is slightly different and requires per-state verification for production confidence.

---

## The doctrine — government edicts

**17 U.S.C. § 105** covers only works of the **US federal** government. State governments are not subject to this specific provision.

However, the US Supreme Court in **Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org (590 U.S. ___, 2020)** extended the common-law "government edicts" doctrine to states. The Court held that works that carry the force of law — statutes enacted by legislatures, rules adopted by agencies, and their official annotations — cannot be copyrighted because they are the authoritative statement of the law that citizens are bound to follow. No one can hold copyright in the law itself.

**Practical result:** State constitutions, state statutes, and binding state administrative rules are almost certainly public domain under this doctrine, just as federal law is.

---

## What is likely public domain for states

- **State constitutions** — the same PD principle as the US Constitution applies.
- **State statutes** — state codes as enacted by the legislature.
- **State supreme court and appellate court opinions** — the judicial branch of each state issues opinions as government edicts; these are generally treated as PD.
- **State administrative rules** as officially promulgated.

---

## The annotation trap — even after Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org

The Georgia case arose specifically because Georgia's official annotated code (the O.C.G.A.) was distributed by a private publisher (LexisNexis) that held copyright in the annotations. The Court held the annotations could not be copyrighted when they were part of the official code.

**But:** A **private publisher's unofficial annotated edition** of a state's statutes (e.g., West's California Codes, McKinney's New York Laws) may carry copyright in the editorial apparatus, headings, selection, and arrangement — even if the underlying statutory text is not copyrightable.

**Rule:** Use raw statutory text from official state government websites (state.gov domains, state legislature official sites), not from private publishers' annotated editions.

---

## State seals and flags

**State seals:** Most states have statutes restricting commercial use of the official state seal. These vary significantly by state. The conservative position is to avoid reproducing any official state seal on a commercial product without checking the specific state's statutes.

**State flags:** State flags are generally not trademarked and the designs themselves are old enough to be public domain. Use of state flag imagery in commercial products is widespread and generally uncontested. However, some states have laws about flag desecration or improper commercial use — check the specific state's law before producing a product that could be read as defacing or mocking the flag.

---

## Practical guidance

For products based on state constitutional or statutory text:

1. Use official state government sources for the text (state legislature or state archives websites).
2. Do not reproduce private publisher annotated editions.
3. Do not reproduce official state seals without checking that state's statutes.
4. For high-volume production decisions, do a 15-minute spot check: search "[state name] official code copyright" to confirm no unusual assertion of copyright.
