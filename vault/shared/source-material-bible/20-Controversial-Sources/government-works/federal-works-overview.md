# Federal Works Overview — 17 U.S.C. § 105 Scope

17 U.S.C. § 105 states that copyright protection is not available for any "work of the United States Government" — defined as a work prepared by an officer or employee of the US Government as part of that person's official duties. This document maps the full scope of that protection.

---

## What § 105 covers

### Statutes and the US Code
The United States Code (all 54+ titles of federal law) is public domain. Any federal statute — the Civil Rights Act, the Sherman Antitrust Act, the Patent Act, the Copyright Act itself — is free to reproduce commercially.

**Practical use:** Legal typography products, civic-education posters, "Know Your Rights" designs, historical-document aesthetics.

### Supreme Court and federal court opinions
All opinions of the US Supreme Court and federal circuit courts are works of the government and are public domain. Landmark decisions — *Brown v. Board of Education*, *Roe v. Wade*, *Miranda v. Arizona*, *Obergefell v. Hodges* — are free to quote or reproduce.

**Practical use:** Legal-history posters, "landmark decisions" print sets, quote typography from famous judicial passages.

**Note:** The parties' briefs, amicus briefs by private lawyers, and exhibits in court cases are NOT government works — only the court's own opinions are.

### Federal agency publications
Reports, guides, factsheets, and publications produced by federal agencies as part of their official duties are government works and are public domain:
- CDC health guidelines
- USDA dietary reports
- NIH research publications (text; some figures may include third-party content)
- NOAA weather/climate data and maps
- USGS topographic and geological maps
- Library of Congress research publications

### NASA images and footage
NASA content is one of the richest PD image libraries on earth. Hubble Space Telescope images, mission photographs, astronaut portraits (official), planetary imagery, rocket launch photographs — all public domain as government works.

**IMPORTANT — the NASA logos:**
- The NASA "meatball" logo (the blue circle with red chevron and white stars) and the "worm" wordmark logo are **NOT freely usable**.
- NASA's logos function as agency identifiers; NASA has published usage guidelines that restrict commercial reproduction that implies endorsement.
- **Rule:** Use NASA *images* freely. Do not use the NASA *logo* on products in a way that implies NASA endorsement of your product.
- Source for NASA imagery: https://images.nasa.gov/

### Military public-affairs photography
Official US military public-affairs photos (taken by military photographers in their official capacity) are government works and public domain. They are widely available through DVIDS (Defense Visual Information Distribution Service: https://www.dvidshub.net/).

**Important:** This does not cover photojournalist images of military subjects — those carry the photographer's copyright. And it does not cover AI-generated simulations that are not actual government photographs.

## What § 105 does NOT cover

### Contractor-created works
If a private contractor was paid by the government to create a work, the contractor may retain copyright unless the contract specifically transferred it to the government. Always verify the provenance of a government-adjacent work if it's not clearly created by a government employee in their official capacity.

### Third-party content in government publications
A government report may contain licensed photographs, charts, or illustrations from private sources. The government's publication does not free that third-party content. If an agency report includes a stock photograph, that photograph remains under the photographer's copyright.

### State and local government works
§ 105 covers **US federal** government works only. State and local governments may hold copyright in their publications (though the "government edicts" doctrine limits this for binding law). See [[state-and-local-works|state-and-local-works]].

### Foreign government works
Works created by foreign governments are not freed by US § 105. They may be protected by copyright both in their home country and in the US.
