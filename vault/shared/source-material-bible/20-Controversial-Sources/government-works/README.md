# Government Works — Rights Overview

US federal government works are public domain by statute. The text of the Constitution, the Declaration of Independence, the Bill of Rights, federal laws, Supreme Court opinions, and federal agency publications are all free to use commercially. This is one of the clearest safe-use categories in the entire Bible.

The trap that sits alongside it: **government seals and insignia are restricted by federal criminal statute**, not by copyright. The text is free. The badge is not.

This subfolder maps both the safe territory and the statutory restrictions.

---

## The governing statute

**17 U.S.C. § 105:** "Copyright protection under this title is not available for any work of the United States Government."

A "work of the United States Government" means a work prepared by an officer or employee of the US Government as part of that person's official duties. The text of any such work is in the public domain from the moment it is created. No registration, no term expiration, no formality — it simply is not copyrightable.

**Scope:**
- The Constitution of the United States and all its amendments
- The Declaration of Independence
- Federal statutes enacted by Congress (the United States Code)
- US Supreme Court opinions and federal court opinions
- Federal agency reports, guidance documents, and publications
- Federal regulatory text (Code of Federal Regulations)
- Congressional records and reports

**Not covered by § 105 (may have copyright):**
- State and local government works (§ 105 covers only federal works; the "government edicts" doctrine from Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org, 2020 extends the principle to states for their binding law, but the precise scope varies — see [[state-and-local-works|state-and-local-works]])
- Works created by contractors working for the government (the contractor retains copyright unless specifically transferred)
- Third-party content embedded in a government publication (a licensed photograph within an agency report is not freed by § 105)

## The seal/insignia statutory restriction

This is the critical counterpoint to § 105's broad freedom.

**18 U.S.C. § 701** prohibits reproducing the seal of the Department of Justice, the FBI, the CIA, and other federal agency seals in a manner that could create the impression of government authorization.

**18 U.S.C. § 713** provides similar protection for the Great Seal of the United States, the Presidential Seal, and seals of executive departments.

**18 U.S.C. § 506** covers false use of government marks.

Military branch insignia are additionally protected by branch-specific statutes (10 U.S.C. §§ 7881, 9774, 8633 and related).

**The practical rule:** Do not reproduce any recognizable federal seal, badge, emblem, or insignia on any commercial product. This is not a copyright question — it is a criminal statute question. The conservative and correct position is a complete prohibition on commercial reproduction of government seals. See [[government-seals-warning|government-seals-warning]] for the detailed treatment.

## The patriotic-content revenue opportunity

Constitutional text, Declaration text, and founding-document excerpts are a proven, high-converting POD niche:

- "We the People" (Preamble to the Constitution) is one of the most recognized phrases in American culture.
- The First Amendment, Second Amendment, and other Bill of Rights provisions have dedicated, passionate buyer segments.
- The Declaration's "We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal..." is universally recognized.
- Quote typography using Lincoln's Gettysburg Address (a federal government speech — PD) performs well.

The content is free. The design is where originality and value are created. See [[brand-safety/patriotic-niche|patriotic-niche]] for the full commercial strategy.
