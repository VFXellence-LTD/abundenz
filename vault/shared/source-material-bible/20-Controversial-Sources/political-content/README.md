# Political Content — Rights & Platform-Safety Overview

Political content is legally and commercially complex. It is mostly legal (the First Amendment provides broad protection for political commentary, satire, and criticism), but "legal" and "commercially viable on major platforms" are two different things. This section maps both.

This subfolder **defers to [[01-Legal-Guidelines/README|01 — Legal Guidelines]]** for all foundational doctrine.

---

## The core tension

**The law** is generally permissive. Creating content about political figures, satirizing politicians, commenting on political events, and expressing political opinions are all broadly protected speech under the First Amendment. Parody and satire of public figures have strong (though not unlimited) legal protection.

**Platform policy** is far more restrictive. Amazon Merch on Demand, Redbubble, Etsy, TeePublic, YouTube, and Meta Ads all impose content policies that restrict political content — often broadly and inconsistently. A product can be entirely legal and still result in a listing takedown or account health penalty. For a business built on these platforms, platform policy is a bigger practical risk than litigation.

---

## The three legal risks in political content

### 1. Right of publicity (living politicians)
Living politicians — like all living persons — have right-of-publicity interests in their name, likeness, and persona. Using a specific politician's name or image on a commercial product (as opposed to editorial or journalistic use) may infringe those rights.

- Right of publicity law varies significantly by state. California, New York, Tennessee, and Indiana have strong protections; some states have almost none.
- Parody and satire have legal shelter but the shelter is not absolute, particularly on commercial products.
- **Safe lane:** Civic/Constitutional content that is not specifically about a named individual. Political speech about issues (Constitutional rights, civic themes, historical events) avoids this risk.
- See [[right-of-publicity|right-of-publicity]] for the full treatment.

### 2. Trademark (party logos, slogans, campaign marks)
Political parties and campaigns trademark their logos, slogans, and branding. Using a trademarked political mark on a commercial product is trademark infringement, not political speech.

- The Republican elephant and Democratic donkey in their specific official designs are trademarked.
- Campaign slogans can be trademarked. "MAKE AMERICA GREAT AGAIN" and "MAGA" have been the subject of multiple USPTO filings.
- "FEEL THE BERN," "I'M WITH HER," and other slogans from recent campaigns have been trademarked or had applications filed.
- **Safe lane:** Generic political themes (liberty, democracy, civic duty, constitutional rights) that use no specific party or campaign marks.
- See [[trademark-traps|trademark-traps]] for the specific marks and current status.

### 3. Platform policy (operational risk regardless of legality)
All major POD and content platforms restrict political content, and their policies are broader than the law requires. A product that is entirely legal can result in a takedown or account flag. See [[platform-policy-risk|platform-policy-risk]].

## What IS viable in the political-content space

Not all political-adjacent content is high-risk:

- **Civic/historical/constitutional themes** (covered in [[government-works/README|government-works]]) — broadly permissive on platforms, large buyer market.
- **Historical political figures** (deceased, no active right-of-publicity estate) — Lincoln, Washington, Jefferson in factual historical use are generally safe; verify per platform.
- **Issue-based content without partisan labels** — "Freedom of Speech," "Constitutional Rights," "Liberty" — broad market appeal, low platform risk.
- **Clearly satirical commentary** with obvious parody framing — legally safer than sincere commercial use of a politician's name/image, but not risk-free.
