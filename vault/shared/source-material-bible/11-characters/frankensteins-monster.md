# Frankenstein's Monster

## Snapshot

The artificial being assembled and reanimated by Victor Frankenstein in Mary Shelley's 1818 novel *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*. In the book he is intelligent, eloquent, and tragic — rejected by his creator and humanity, he turns to vengeance. (Note: "Frankenstein" is the *creator's* name; the creature is unnamed.)

## Copyright status (the text/expression)

Fully public domain worldwide. *Frankenstein* was first published in 1818 (and revised 1831); both editions are PD. Mary Shelley died in 1851 (life+70 expired 1921). The novel, the creature, Victor Frankenstein, Elizabeth, the De Lacey family, and all plot are free to use.

## What is RESTRICTED (the modern design / trademark / publicity)

This is the single clearest teaching case in the whole library:

- **The Karloff / Universal 1931 design is RESTRICTED.** The flat-topped square head, the protruding brow, the two neck electrodes ("bolts"), the heavy-lidded eyes, the greenish scarred skin, the too-short jacket — this is **Jack Pierce's makeup design for Boris Karloff in Universal's 1931 film**. It is protected by Universal's copyright in the film and is actively used as a Universal trademark on merchandise. **None of these features appear in Shelley's novel.** Do not draw, sculpt, or AI-generate this look.
- **Right of publicity.** Karloff's specific facial likeness in the makeup is additionally a publicity-rights concern.
- **Shelley's PD description is entirely different and safe:** roughly eight feet tall, with translucent yellowish skin barely concealing the muscles and blood vessels beneath, watery dull-yellow eyes, straight black lips, and long lustrous black hair and pearly white teeth that contrast horribly with the rest. He is articulate, well-read, and philosophically eloquent.

## Risk level and why

**Caution.** The text is bulletproof; the danger is that "Frankenstein" is mentally synonymous with the restricted Universal design, so creators and AI models default to bolts and flat heads. Because Universal both copyrights the film design and trademarks the merch image, copying it is a double-barrel risk. Working from Shelley's description is fully safe and also more distinctive.

## Safe-use guidance

- **Do:** Depict Shelley's creature — gaunt, towering, sutured, translucent-skinned, dark-haired, soulful and articulate. Use the novel's whole cast and story, including the creature narrating his own tale. Call the creator "Victor Frankenstein" or "Frankenstein"; call the being "the creature/the monster" (it is unnamed).
- **Don't:** Add flat-top head, neck bolts, green skin, or the Karloff face. Don't reproduce Universal's merch image. Don't market under the Universal Monsters brand.

## Creative opportunities

### Design / POD
"The Modern Prometheus" quote posters, anatomical line-art creature studies, alpine-ice gothic scenes, "We are all monsters to someone" tees — all using Shelley-faithful (or wholly original) creature designs that deliberately avoid the bolts. The novel-true look is a market differentiator.

### Story / writing
The creature's first-person memoir, a bioethics reframe (cloning/AI), a sympathetic children's "misunderstood creature" version, an audio-drama. The text is fully remixable.

### Other applications
Spooky-season POD, AI-writing prompt packs for "the creator's regret" themes, a Z-named original reanimated-being character for a branded line that needs its own trademark and a clean original silhouette.
