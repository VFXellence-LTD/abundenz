# Safe to Use

> Material in these categories is genuinely free to build on commercially — as long as you respect the edges noted under each. This is the green-light bucket.

## Summary

"Safe" means you can adapt, illustrate, print, sell, and remix the material for commercial products without a license. It does **not** mean "ignore all rules" — each category has a specific reason it is free and a specific edge that can pull a particular instance out of the safe zone. Always use the **original old expression**, not a modern repackaging of it.

---

## 1. Public-domain literary & artistic works (copyright expired)

**Why safe:** The copyright term has run out, so the expression belongs to everyone. In the US (2026), this means works **published 1930 or earlier** (see [[us-public-domain-cutoff]]).

**Examples:** *Frankenstein* (Mary Shelley, 1818), *Dracula* (Bram Stoker, 1897), *Pride and Prejudice* (Austen, 1813), *The Wonderful Wizard of Oz* (Baum, 1900 book), Sherlock Holmes stories published up to 1930, *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* (Carroll, 1865), the works of Shakespeare, Poe, Dickens, the Brontës, Jules Verne, H.G. Wells (pre-1931 titles).

**The edge:** Only the **old expression** is free. A modern **translation**, a modern **critical/annotated edition**, a specific **illustration**, or a film/stage **adaptation** carries its own fresh copyright. Use the original-language 1818 text of *Frankenstein*, not a 2015 translation; draw your own monster, do not copy Universal's 1931 design. See [[pd-source-vs-modern-adaptation]]. Also still run the trademark/publicity checks — a PD character can be a live trademark.

---

## 2. Public-domain illustrations & artworks (copyright expired)

**Why safe:** Same expiry logic applied to images. Old illustrations, paintings, prints, and engravings whose copyright has expired are free to reproduce and adapt.

**Examples:** John Tenniel's original *Alice* illustrations (1865), Gustave Doré engravings, Audubon bird prints, Japanese ukiyo-e woodblock prints (Hokusai's *Great Wave*, c.1831), Art Nouveau posters by Mucha (pre-1931), botanical and anatomical plates from old encyclopedias, Old Master paintings (Vermeer, Rembrandt, etc.).

**The edge:** A modern **photograph** of a public-domain painting can carry its own copyright in some jurisdictions (though US law — *Bridgeman v. Corel* — says a faithful 2D reproduction lacks originality and is **not** separately copyrightable; other countries differ). Prefer scans/reproductions explicitly marked PD or CC0 (e.g. from the Met, Rijksmuseum, NYPL, Smithsonian Open Access). Modern restorations/colorizations may add a protectable layer.

---

## 3. Mythology, legend & religious texts (ancient — never copyrighted)

**Why safe:** These predate copyright entirely. Greek, Roman, Norse, Egyptian, Hindu, Celtic, Mesopotamian, and other ancient myths are part of humanity's common heritage. Foundational religious texts (the Bible's underlying ancient text, the Quran's text, the Vedas, etc.) are ancient.

**Examples:** Zeus, Odin, Anubis, Thor (the myth, not Marvel's), Medusa, the Minotaur, Ragnarök, the Trojan War, King Arthur and the Round Table (medieval legend), Beowulf, the Epic of Gilgamesh, Aesop's fables.

**The edge:** A **specific modern retelling, translation, or visual design** of a myth is copyrighted. The *idea* of Thor is free; **Marvel's Thor** (costume, look, characterization) and **God of War's Kratos-vs-gods** designs are protected. A modern English translation of *The Odyssey* is copyrighted; the ancient Greek is not. See [[pd-source-vs-modern-adaptation]] and the mythology sections of the Bible. Modern **scripture translations** (e.g. NIV, ESV) are copyrighted; older ones (KJV original text) are PD.

---

## 4. Folklore, fairy tales & traditional culture (traditional — no single author)

**Why safe:** Traditional folk tales, fairy tales, folk songs, nursery rhymes, and traditional motifs have no identifiable living author and predate or sit outside copyright.

**Examples:** Cinderella, Snow White, Little Red Riding Hood, Hansel & Gretel, Jack and the Beanstalk, the Brothers Grimm and Hans Christian Andersen tales (early-1800s texts), Mother Goose rhymes, Anansi stories, traditional folk songs.

**The edge:** **Disney's specific versions** of these tales are heavily protected — Disney's Cinderella dress/design, Disney's Little Mermaid (Ariel) look, Disney's Snow White design are all copyrighted and trademarked. The *tale* is free; the *Disney visual* is not. See [[do-not-use]] and [[pd-source-vs-modern-adaptation]]. Be mindful of **cultural sensitivity** with living indigenous traditions — legality and ethics can diverge.

---

## 5. Historical symbols, heraldry & old designs

**Why safe:** Ancient and historical symbols, heraldic devices, and old decorative patterns are not owned by anyone.

**Examples:** Celtic knots, the Ankh, the Ouroboros, the Tree of Life, alchemical symbols, zodiac/astrological signs, classical heraldry, Greek key/meander patterns, fleur-de-lis, historic flags of the distant past, runic alphabets (the script, used non-deceptively).

**The edge:** Some symbols are **hate symbols** or have been weaponized — avoid those entirely regardless of historical origin (see [[do-not-use]], hate symbols). Some "old-looking" symbols are actually **modern trademarks or org logos** (e.g. a specific fraternity crest, a modern brand's stylized rune). And **national flags/emblems** can carry use restrictions in some countries.

---

## 6. US Federal Government works (17 U.S.C. § 105)

**Why safe:** Works created by **US federal government employees as part of their official duties are not subject to copyright** and are in the public domain immediately.

**Examples:** NASA images and footage, USGS maps, NOAA weather/satellite imagery, US military public-affairs photos, Library of Congress's own works, federal agency reports, US patent drawings, official US government documents and seals' descriptive text, NPS (National Park Service) public-domain photography.

**The edge:** This applies to **US federal** works, **not** state/local government (which can hold copyright), **not** foreign governments, and **not** contractor-created works (a private contractor paid by the government may retain copyright). NASA's *logos and insignia* (the "meatball" and "worm") have **separate usage guidelines and can function as trademarks/identifiers** — using them to imply endorsement is restricted. Third-party material embedded in a government work (a licensed photo in a report) is not freed.

---

## 7. CC0 / Public Domain Dedication

**Why safe:** Creative Commons Zero (CC0) and similar public-domain dedications are the creator **voluntarily waiving all rights**. The work is functionally public domain — usable for any purpose including commercial, no attribution required.

**Examples:** CC0 assets on Unsplash (older uploads — note license changed), Pexels, Pixabay (verify per-asset), the Met Open Access (CC0), Rijksmuseum, Smithsonian Open Access, many datasets and fonts on Google Fonts (often SIL OFL, which is open but not CC0).

**The edge:** **Verify the specific asset's license** — platforms change terms (Unsplash moved to its own license; Pixabay/Pexels have their own terms with restrictions on identifiable people and selling unmodified copies). CC0 waives the *uploader's* copyright but cannot waive **third-party rights** baked into the content — a CC0 photo of a celebrity still has right-of-publicity issues; a CC0 photo containing a visible brand logo still has trademark issues.

---

## 8. Permissively/openly licensed works (CC-BY, CC-BY-SA, OFL, MIT, etc.)

**Why safe (conditionally):** Open licenses grant broad permission to use commercially, **provided you follow the license terms.** This is "safe" only if you actually comply.

**Examples:** **CC-BY** (use freely, must attribute), **CC-BY-SA** (attribute + share-alike: derivatives must use the same license), **SIL Open Font License** (use fonts freely, including commercially, with conditions on redistribution and renaming), code under MIT/Apache.

**The edge — read the license, every time:**
- **NonCommercial (CC-BY-NC):** **NOT safe for our commercial products.** Excludes commercial use entirely → treat as `caution`/`avoid` for Polymath.
- **NoDerivatives (CC-BY-ND):** you may not adapt/remix → bad fit for a remix pipeline.
- **ShareAlike (CC-BY-SA):** forces your derivative to be licensed the same way — may be incompatible with selling closed products. Treat as `caution`.
- **Attribution** is mandatory on CC-BY/-SA: failing to attribute is a license breach that turns a safe use into infringement.

> **Polymath rule:** Only **CC0**, **CC-BY** (with attribution), and **OFL fonts** (with compliance) are routinely safe for commercial products. **CC-BY-NC and CC-BY-ND are not** usable for our commercial output. CC-BY-SA is `caution`.

## How an AI should apply this

A candidate maps to `risk_level: safe` only if **all** hold:
1. It falls into one of the categories above (PD work / PD image / ancient myth / traditional folklore / historical symbol / US-federal work / CC0 / compliant CC-BY/OFL).
2. You are using the **original old expression**, not a modern translation/illustration/adaptation/recording.
3. The trademark check passes (no live brand/source confusion) — see [[copyright-vs-trademark-vs-likeness]].
4. The publicity check passes (no recognizable real person in a commercial use).
5. For any license-based source, the **specific license permits commercial + derivative use** and you will comply (attribution, etc.).

If any of these is uncertain, drop to [[use-with-caution]].

## Examples

- **SAFE:** Illustrate your own Medusa for a poster — ancient myth, your own art → `safe`.
- **SAFE:** Reprint Tenniel's 1865 Alice engravings on a tote → PD illustration → `safe`.
- **SAFE:** Use a NASA Hubble image on a t-shirt → US federal work, PD → `safe` (don't add the NASA logo).
- **SAFE (with attribution):** A CC-BY photo of a mountain → `safe` if you credit the photographer.
- **NOT SAFE:** A CC-BY-NC illustration on merch you sell → `avoid` (NonCommercial).
- **NOT SAFE:** Disney's Cinderella dress design "because Cinderella is a fairy tale" → `avoid` (Disney's specific design is protected).
