# Use With Caution

> The yellow-light bucket. These can sometimes be used, but only in narrow ways, and the safe path is fact-specific. "Caution" never means "go ahead" — it means "stop and think, and probably restrict or avoid."

## Summary

Items here are not automatically off-limits, but they are not free either. Usability depends entirely on **how** you use them: as expressive commentary vs as a brand; transformed vs copied; the old PD layer vs a newer protected layer. The default posture is restraint — design *around* the protected element, not into it.

---

## 1. Trademarks (brand names, logos, slogans, mascots)

**Why caution:** Trademarks protect commercial source identifiers and **do not expire** (see [[copyright-vs-trademark-vs-likeness]]). The legal test is **likelihood of consumer confusion** about source, sponsorship, or affiliation.

**What's risky:** Putting a brand name/logo on merch (implies endorsement); using a mascot; using trade dress (a distinctive product look); using a slogan as your own tagline.

**The narrow safe lane:** *Nominative / descriptive* references — naming a brand to talk *about* it (reviews, commentary, comparison) — and genuinely *transformative parody* can be defensible. But on **physical merchandise for sale**, almost any brand use creates source-confusion exposure. Treat brand-on-product as `avoid`.

**AI rule:** Brand name/logo/mascot in product art → `avoid`. Brand named in editorial/commentary text, accurately and without implying endorsement → `caution`.

---

## 2. Character likenesses from in-copyright works

**Why caution:** A fictional character can be protected by **copyright** (if sufficiently delineated) **and/or trademark** (if used as a brand). Characters from works still in copyright are protected.

**Examples:** Harry Potter, James Bond, Katniss Everdeen, Jack Reacher, most comic/film/TV characters created after 1930.

**The narrow lane:** Almost none for commercial merch. Brief, transformative editorial commentary may be defensible; commercial product use is not. → effectively `avoid` for products.

**AI rule:** Named in-copyright character used as product subject → `avoid`. Discussed in a blog/podcast critically → `caution`.

---

## 3. Modern adaptations of public-domain works

**Why caution:** When a PD story is re-adapted, the **new creative additions** in that adaptation get fresh copyright, even though the old core is free. The *2005 film script* of a PD novel, a *new character* added to a fairy tale, a *specific modern visual design* — all protected.

**Examples:** A modern screenplay of *A Christmas Carol*; the BBC *Sherlock* characterization and visual style; a contemporary illustrated *Alice* edition; added songs/dialogue in a stage musical of a PD tale.

**The safe lane:** Go back to the **original PD source** and create your **own** new adaptation from it. Do **not** copy the modern adaptation's *added* elements. See [[pd-source-vs-modern-adaptation]].

**AI rule:** Build from the PD original → `safe`. Borrow distinctive elements unique to a modern adaptation → `avoid`.

---

## 4. Derivative works generally

**Why caution:** A derivative work (translation, abridgment, arrangement, adaptation, colorization, remaster, annotated edition) carries its **own** copyright in the *new* contributions even when the underlying work is PD.

**Examples:** A modern translation of *The Odyssey*; a colorized version of an old black-and-white film; a critical edition with scholarly notes; a remastered/cleaned-up audio recording; a sheet-music arrangement of a PD melody.

**The safe lane:** Use the **underlying PD layer directly** (the ancient Greek, the original B&W footage, the original melody). Avoid the value-added modern layer.

**AI rule:** Underlying PD layer used directly → `safe`. Modern derivative layer copied → `caution`/`avoid`.

---

## 5. Estate-controlled properties

**Why caution:** Even when copyright is winding down, an author's **estate** may aggressively assert remaining copyrights (on later works), trademarks, and publicity rights, and is willing to litigate. The chilling effect is real even where the legal claim is thin.

**Examples:** The **Conan Doyle Estate** (asserted that late Sherlock Holmes stories — and the "more emotional" Holmes traits in them — remained protected until those specific stories entered PD); the **Tolkien Estate** (fierce trademark/IP enforcement around Middle-earth names); the **Edgar Rice Burroughs** estate (Tarzan, John Carter — trademarks); the **Hemingway**, **Fitzgerald**, **Dr. Seuss** (Seuss Enterprises) estates.

**The safe lane:** Use only the clearly-PD early works and avoid anything still under estate control or trademarked names. Expect enforcement and design well clear.

**AI rule:** Use a property under active estate enforcement → `caution`; if a trademarked name or post-cutoff work is involved → `avoid`.

---

## 6. Licensed franchises

**Why caution:** Franchises (films, games, toy lines, comics universes) are dense bundles of overlapping copyrights and trademarks, heavily monetized through official licensing programs, and policed hard. There is essentially no unlicensed commercial lane.

**Examples:** Star Wars, Marvel/DC, Pokémon, Harry Potter/Wizarding World, Disney/Pixar, Nintendo properties, *Lord of the Rings*, *Game of Thrones*.

**The safe lane:** None for unlicensed commercial products. Reference in genuine editorial/critical content only.

**AI rule:** Franchise asset in a product → `avoid`. Mentioned in commentary → `caution`.

---

## 7. Brand identifiers & trade dress

**Why caution:** Beyond logos, brands protect distinctive **visual identity**: signature color combinations, packaging shapes, store layouts, characteristic typography-as-brand, distinctive product silhouettes (the Coke bottle shape).

**Examples:** Tiffany blue (a registered color mark), the Coca-Cola bottle contour, a specific stripe pattern (Adidas three stripes), a signature checkmark, distinctive packaging.

**The safe lane:** Use generic design language, not a specific brand's protected trade dress. A teal box is fine; replicating Tiffany's exact blue as your brand color is not.

**AI rule:** Replicate identifiable brand trade dress → `avoid`. Use generic styling inspired by an era/genre → `safe`.

---

## 8. Right of publicity (real people)

**Why caution:** Real, identifiable people have rights in their name, likeness, voice, and persona, strongest against **commercial** use. Applies to living people and, in many jurisdictions, to the deceased for decades.

**Examples:** Any recognizable celebrity, athlete, musician, politician (as a person), historical figures whose estates manage publicity (Elvis, Marilyn Monroe, Einstein — Einstein's likeness is actively licensed). Even a **sound-alike** voice or a clear **look-alike** can infringe.

**The narrow lane:** Genuinely **newsworthy, biographical, or transformative editorial** use of public figures can be defensible (a critical article, factual reporting). Long-dead historical figures with no active estate (e.g. Abraham Lincoln, Cleopatra) are generally usable. **Commercial merch of a recognizable living/recent person → `avoid`.**

**AI rule:** Recognizable real person on a product → `avoid`. Long-dead historical figure, no active estate, used factually → `caution`/`safe`. AI-generated "person who looks like [celebrity]" → `avoid`.

---

## 9. AI-generated content that resembles protected work

**Why caution:** AI image/video/text tools can output material that closely mimics a copyrighted style, a specific character, a real person, or a trademarked design. The fact that an AI produced it does **not** clear the underlying rights.

**Examples:** "In the style of [living artist]" outputs; an AI image that reproduces a recognizable character; an AI voice that mimics a real performer; AI text that reproduces substantial protected passages.

**The safe lane:** Prompt toward **PD sources, generic genres, historical movements, and original concepts**; never toward a named living artist, named character, named celebrity, or specific protected work. Also note: purely AI-generated output may have **thin or no copyright protection of its own** (US Copyright Office position), which affects whether *you* can stop others copying it — a separate business concern.

**AI rule:** Prompt references a protected entity → `avoid` the prompt. Prompt references PD/generic/original material → `safe`.

---

## How an AI should apply this

For anything in this bucket:
1. Identify **which protected layer** is in play (trademark? a modern adaptation's additions? an estate's later works? a real person?).
2. Ask: is the use **commercial product** or **editorial commentary**? Commercial product use pushes strongly toward `avoid`.
3. Ask: am I copying the **protected element** or designing **around** it from the PD/original/generic base? Designing around → can reach `safe`.
4. If the answer is "commercial product use of the protected element" → `avoid`.
5. If genuinely transformative / editorial / from-the-PD-original → `caution` (document the reasoning) or `safe`.

When uncertain, set `risk_level: caution`, write the specific danger into `risk_notes`, and prefer the safe redesign.

## Examples

- **CAUTION→SAFE:** Write your own modern retelling of *A Christmas Carol* from Dickens' 1843 text → `safe` (don't copy a specific film's additions).
- **CAUTION→AVOID:** Put "Sherlock Holmes" deerstalker art on a mug using the BBC *Sherlock* look → `avoid` (modern adaptation + possible trademark).
- **CAUTION:** Blog post critically reviewing the new Star Wars film, naming it → `caution` (editorial, fine; don't sell merch).
- **AVOID:** A t-shirt of a famous living musician's face → `avoid` (right of publicity).
- **AVOID:** "An illustration in the style of [named living illustrator]" for sale → `avoid`.
