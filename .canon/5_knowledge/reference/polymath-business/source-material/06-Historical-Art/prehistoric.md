# Prehistoric Art (Cave Painting & Petroglyphs)

> The oldest human image-making: ochre animals and stenciled hands on rock walls, c.40,000–3,000 BCE — raw, elemental mark-making with no agenda but to record and conjure.

## Overview
Prehistoric art spans Paleolithic cave painting (Lascaux, Chauvet, Altamira), Neolithic petroglyphs, and Aboriginal rock art. It is the deepest well of "primal mark" imagery available, and because the works are tens of thousands of years old, the imagery is entirely free of copyright. As a design language it reads as origin, instinct, and the human urge to make a mark.

## Defining Features
- Imagery drawn directly on uneven rock, following the surface rather than fighting it.
- Earth pigments only: charcoal black, red and yellow ochre, white.
- Animals depicted with astonishing economy and movement; humans rendered as simple stick or silhouette figures.
- Hands stenciled by blowing pigment around a hand pressed to the wall.
- No perspective, no ground line, no frame — figures float and overlap.

## Visual Vocabulary
Flat silhouettes and outline drawings; layered, overlapping animals; repeated dots and lines; negative-space handprints. Forms are confident and gestural, never fussy. Composition is accumulative — images added over generations, so density and overlap read as authentic.

## Shapes
Organic animal silhouettes, simple stick figures, open hand shapes, dots, dashes, concentric spirals, zigzags, grids.

## Textures
Rough stone grain showing through pigment, dry-brush charcoal, blown-pigment soft edges around stencils, granular ochre wash.

## Palette
The "earth ochre" family: charcoal black, burnt sienna, red ochre, yellow ochre, bone white, and the warm grey-brown of rock. Restricted, warm, and grounding. See [[08-Color-Palettes/README]] for the codified earth palette.

## Key Motifs
Charging or grazing herds (bison, horse, deer, mammoth), the stenciled handprint, the lone hunter with spear, sun and star marks, dot constellations, the spiral.

## Rights Note
The STYLE is free to emulate and the original works are public domain — they are millennia old and were never under copyright. The only real-world caution is that a **modern photograph** of a cave or rock-art site can itself be copyrighted by the photographer, and some heritage sites assert image-use terms. So do not lift a specific contemporary photo of Lascaux; instead illustrate or generate cave-style imagery yourself. See [[01-Legal-Guidelines/safe-to-use]].
