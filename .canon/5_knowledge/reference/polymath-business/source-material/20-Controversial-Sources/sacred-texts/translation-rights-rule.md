# Translation Rights Rule — Ancient Text PD ≠ Translation PD

> The single most important rule in the sacred-texts subfolder. Read this before any other entry here.

---

## The doctrine

**Ancient original text is public domain. Each translation is an independent copyrighted work.**

When a translator renders the Hebrew Torah, Greek New Testament, Arabic Quran, Sanskrit Gita, or Pali Dhammapada into English, that translator's choices — word selection, phrasing, sentence structure, interpretive decisions — constitute original creative expression. That expression is copyrightable.

Under US copyright law, a translation is a "derivative work" (17 U.S.C. § 101). The underlying work (the ancient text) may be in the public domain, but the derivative work (the translation) carries its own copyright from the moment of creation, with a term measured from the translator's death or the publication date depending on when and how it was published.

---

## The practical trap

Here is the trap in concrete terms:

- The King James Version (1611) and the New International Version (1978) are both English Bibles.
- A verse from the KJV and the "same" verse from the NIV may look nearly identical to a non-specialist.
- The KJV is **public domain** in the US. The NIV is **actively copyrighted** by Biblica.
- Printing the NIV text on a t-shirt without a license is **copyright infringement**.
- Printing the KJV text on a t-shirt is **legally clear**.

The same structure applies to every sacred text in every faith tradition. The original language is free; the translation you are looking at is almost certainly not — unless it is old enough that its own term has expired.

---

## How to determine whether a translation is PD

Apply the standard US public domain rules from [[01-Legal-Guidelines/us-public-domain-cutoff]]:

**For US publication before 1978:**
- If published **1930 or earlier** (as of 2026): **public domain**. The 95-year term has expired.
- If published **1931–1977**: may still be in copyright (95-year rule; e.g. a 1940 translation is PD only after Jan 1 2036). **Do not assume PD.**
- If published **1978 or later**: life of translator + 70 years. Not PD for decades.

**For works by individual translators (not corporate authors):**
- Also apply the life+70 rule. If the translator died more than 70 years ago, AND the work was published outside the 95-year window trap, the copyright has expired.

**Practical shortcut:** If the translation was **published before 1931 and the translator died before 1956**, you are almost certainly safe in the US. Verify the specific work anyway.

## What "using the translation" means for products

Any product that reproduces or paraphrases the text of a translation in a recognizable way is using the translation:

- Printing a verse verbatim on a t-shirt, poster, mug, or card.
- Using the phrasing of a verse as a design element (even partial — "For God so loved the world" in the NIV phrasing differs subtly from the KJV "For God so loved the world" and both can trigger a claim if the protected version is close enough).
- Overlaying translated text in an AI-generated image.
- Reading from a copyrighted translation in a podcast or video without a license.

**The safe rule:** For commercial products, use **only** translations that are verifiably public domain. The specific safe editions for each faith are listed in the per-faith entries in this subfolder. When in doubt, consult the source work's entry and confirm `verified: true` and `pd_status: public-domain` before production.

## Modern study Bibles, annotated editions, and commentary

Even if the base translation text is public domain (e.g. KJV), a **modern annotated study Bible** may add copyrighted layer:

- Study notes, commentary, cross-references, and introductions written by a modern scholar are copyrighted even if the underlying scripture text is not.
- Use only the bare scripture text from a PD translation — not the packaging, commentary, or study apparatus of a modern edition.
- The physical typeset layout of a modern KJV edition may carry its own thin copyright in the layout/design (not the text). Print the *text*, not a scan of a modern printed page.

This mirrors the Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org (2020) principle: even official annotated legal codes can carry copyright in the annotations while the underlying statute text is free.

## General rule for all faiths

> Check the translator's name and the publication year. If the publication year is 1930 or earlier, you are likely safe in the US. If later, verify specifically. When uncertain, treat as `risk_level: caution` or `avoid` and use a confirmed-PD alternative.
