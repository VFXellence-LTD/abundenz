# Government Seals & Insignia — DO NOT USE

> This is the statutory restriction that sits alongside the broad freedom of 17 U.S.C. § 105. The text is free; the seal is not. These are separate legal questions governed by separate statutes.

---

## The doctrine

**17 U.S.C. § 105** establishes that works of the US federal government carry no copyright. This means the text of the Constitution, federal statutes, court opinions, and agency publications is public domain.

**18 U.S.C. §§ 506, 701, 713** establish that specific government seals, badges, and emblems cannot be reproduced in commerce in ways that create a false impression of federal authorization — **regardless of copyright**. This is a criminal statute, not a civil intellectual property question.

The two rules apply to different things. The first frees text. The second restricts seals. Both apply simultaneously.

---

## What is restricted

### The Great Seal of the United States
The two-sided seal (eagle on obverse; pyramid and "Annuit Coeptis / Novus Ordo Seclorum" on reverse). Restricted under 18 U.S.C. § 713.

**Note:** The *design elements* of the Great Seal — the eagle, the arrows, the olive branch, the thirteen stars, the shield — are ancient symbols that have appeared in American iconography for 250 years and are not individually restricted. A stylized American eagle that looks nothing like the specific Great Seal design is fine. Reproducing the Great Seal itself as an official-looking emblem on a product is not.

### The Presidential Seal
The seal of the Office of the President of the United States. Restricted under 18 U.S.C. § 713.

### Department and agency seals
18 U.S.C. § 701 restricts use of seals, emblems, and names of certain federal agencies and departments:
- Department of Justice seal
- FBI seal and badge
- Secret Service seal
- Other federal law-enforcement agency seals
- Department of Defense

**18 U.S.C. § 706** specifically covers the Department of State seal.

Additional statutory protections cover the CIA, NSA, and other intelligence community agency seals.

### Military branch insignia
Each branch of the US military protects its insignia under separate statutes:
- Army (10 U.S.C. § 7881)
- Navy and Marine Corps (related statutes)
- Air Force (10 U.S.C. § 9774)
- Coast Guard
- Space Force

Military insignia includes branch seals, rank insignia, unit crests, and official badges. Using military branch insignia on commercial merchandise without a license agreement with that branch is restricted.

---

## What IS free (important distinction)

The restrictions apply to official seals and insignia, not to:

- The *text* of documents that use a seal as a heading (the text of a document bearing the Presidential Seal is free; reproducing the seal itself is not).
- Historical engravings and illustrations of seals published before copyright would have applied — though even here, be cautious about creating a false impression of authorization.
- General American symbolic vocabulary: the bald eagle as a national symbol (not the specific Great Seal design), the American flag design, stars and stripes, the Liberty Bell. These are free to use.
- The *names* of government agencies: writing "Federal Bureau of Investigation" in text is free; reproducing the FBI seal and badge is not.

## Why this matters for patriotic-content products

The patriotic-content niche is one of the highest-revenue opportunities in this section. The path through it is clear:

- **Safe:** Constitution text, Declaration text, eagle imagery, American flag design, "We the People" typography, patriotic color schemes, stars-and-stripes motifs.
- **Not safe:** Presidential Seal, FBI badge, military branch seals, Great Seal reproduced as an official emblem.

A common trap: designing a "constitutional rights" product that includes, for aesthetic completeness, the Great Seal or an official-looking government badge. The text is fine; the seal is not. Remove the seal.

Another common trap: "military branch tribute" products that reproduce the exact official seal or badge of a military branch. These require a formal licensing agreement with the specific branch's public-affairs office. Without that agreement, do not use official seals or badges.
