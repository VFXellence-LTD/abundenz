# Right of Publicity — Living Politicians & Public Figures

The right of publicity is the legal right of an individual to control commercial use of their name, image, likeness, and persona. In the US, it is governed by state law — there is no federal right-of-publicity statute — which means the protection varies significantly depending on the state in which the claim arises.

---

## The general rule

**Using a living politician's name, face, or distinctive persona on a commercial product creates right-of-publicity risk.**

This applies even when the use is legal under First Amendment analysis for editorial or political speech — because "commercial product" changes the analysis. The First Amendment protects political speech, including about public figures. It does not freely permit commercial exploitation of a person's identity.

The basic test most courts apply: Is the primary purpose **expressive** (commenting on, critiquing, satirizing the person as a public figure) or **commercial** (using the person's identity to sell a product)? Commercial products on POD platforms are almost always primarily commercial, even when the content has an expressive dimension.

---

## State-by-state variation

Right-of-publicity law is fragmented because it is state law. Key variations:

**Strong protection (high risk for commercial use):**
- **California** — broad right of publicity by statute (Cal. Civ. Code § 3344) and common law; politicians are covered; heirs can assert rights for 70 years post-death.
- **New York** — statutory protection (NY Civil Rights Law §§ 50-51); covers use of name, portrait, picture, or voice "for advertising purposes or for purposes of trade."
- **Tennessee** — very strong (Tennessee's "Personal Rights Protection Act" / the "Elvis Act" — protects against commercial exploitation, survives death).
- **Indiana** — strong post-mortem rights.

**Moderate protection:**
- **Texas**, **Florida**, **Illinois** — statutory protections with varying scope.

**Weak or uncertain protection:**
- Many states have no statute and uncertain common-law protection.

**Practical result:** Because VFXellence operates nationally (products ship nationwide, sold on national platforms), the most protective state's law can apply to any given transaction. California and New York cover enormous market shares. Treat all living politicians as having strong right-of-publicity protection.

---

## What about parody and satire?

Parody and satire of public figures (including politicians) have First Amendment protection. But:

1. The parody must be **clear** — a product that looks like a sincere endorsement or merchandise line rather than an obvious joke is not protected parody.
2. Commercial products for sale exist in a difficult zone. Courts have found that selling a satirical product is a "commercial use" that can override the parody defense.
3. Platform content moderation does not analyze parody defenses — automated systems and human reviewers at Amazon Merch, Redbubble, etc. will remove clearly political content whether or not it would survive a legal challenge.

**The practical conclusion:** Even if you are legally confident your parody product would survive a right-of-publicity claim, the platform takedown risk makes it commercially unviable on major platforms without accepting regular disruptions.

---

## Historical/deceased figures

Deceased politicians present a different calculus:

- **Long deceased (pre-1956 death date):** No right-of-publicity issues in most states. Historical figures like Abraham Lincoln, George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, FDR, and others are free to use in commercial contexts.
- **Recently deceased (post-1956):** Some states (particularly California, Tennessee, Indiana) extend right-of-publicity rights to heirs for 70 years post-death. A politician who died in 1990 may still have heir-held publicity rights in California until 2060. Research each case.
- **Active political legacy:** Even historically deceased figures whose estate or family actively maintains a brand/persona may push back. Check whether an estate is asserting rights before commercial use of a recently-deceased figure.

---

## The safe lane for political-themed products

To avoid right-of-publicity risk entirely:

- Focus on **civic and constitutional themes** rather than specific politicians: "First Amendment Rights" rather than a specific politician; "We the People" rather than a President's image.
- Use **historical figures deceased long before 1955** in historical/educational framing.
- Create **issue-based content** (liberty, freedom, constitutional rights) with no identifiable individual's name or likeness.
