# 20 — Controversial Sources: Rights & Brand-Safety Guide

This section is the rights and brand-safety guide for politically, religiously, and culturally sensitive source material that carries **significant revenue potential alongside significant legal or platform risk**. It is not a list of things to avoid — it is a map of exactly what you *can* build on and precisely what you must not touch.

This section **defers to [[01-Legal-Guidelines/README|01 — Legal Guidelines]]** for all foundational legal doctrine. If anything here conflicts with that section, section 01 wins.

---

## Why this section exists

Many of the highest-revenue niches in POD and content marketing sit in "controversial" territory:

- **Faith content** (KJV scripture, Christian-themed apparel, devotional quotes) is one of the largest and most loyal consumer segments in the US market.
- **Patriotic content** (Constitutional text, "We the People," founding-document excerpts) has enduring, cross-political demand.
- **Political commentary** generates viral traffic and strong audience affinity, though it carries the highest platform-policy risk.

The material is not inherently dangerous — but it is easy to get wrong in ways that trigger takedowns, account bans, or legal action. This guide exists so operators and AI systems can navigate to the real revenue while staying out of the traps.

This section uses the same three-tier model as [[01-Legal-Guidelines/README]]:

In this section, **"safe" is context-dependent** in ways that differ from other Bible sections. Government text is legally safe but a specific government seal is statutorily prohibited. A KJV verse is copyright-safe but the same verse from a modern translation is not. These distinctions are the entire point of this guide.

## The revenue case for controversial niches

The faith niche and patriotic niche are not edge cases — they are two of the most commercially durable POD niches in the English-speaking market. Faith-niche buyers are brand-loyal, purchase in volume (gifts, home decor, apparel), and respond well to typographic products (scripture verses, devotional quotes). Patriotic buyers are similarly loyal and broadly bipartisan when the content is civic/historical rather than partisan.

**The path to safe revenue:** Use public-domain source material (KJV Bible text, Constitutional text, Declaration of Independence) in **original designs** that do not incorporate any protected element (modern translations, government seals, living politicians' likenesses, party marks). This entire section maps that path.

**The brand-isolation principle:** controversial niches — especially political and faith-specific content — should operate under a **dedicated brand separate from the main Polymath/VFXellence storefront**. See [[brand-safety/niche-isolation-playbook|niche-isolation-playbook]] for the rationale and how-to.

## Critical legal doctrines this section covers

### 1. US government works — text is free; seals are statutory (not copyright) traps
**17 U.S.C. § 105:** Works created by US federal government employees in their official duties carry no copyright. The Constitution, Declaration of Independence, Bill of Rights, federal statutes, Supreme Court opinions, and federal agency publications are all public domain from the moment of creation. The *text* is completely free to print on any product.

The trap: **government seals and insignia** are restricted not by copyright but by **federal statute** (18 U.S.C. §§ 506, 701, 713). The FBI seal, CIA seal, Presidential Seal, Great Seal of the United States, and military insignia cannot be reproduced in a manner likely to cause confusion about authorization. The *words* of the Constitution are free. The *Presidential Seal* is not. See [[government-works/government-seals-warning|government-seals-warning]].

### 2. Sacred texts — ancient original is PD; every translation is its own copyright
The original Hebrew Tanakh, Greek New Testament, Arabic Quran, Sanskrit Vedas, and Pali Dhammapada are ancient and uncopyrightable. But every translation is an independent creative work with its own copyright term. The KJV (1611) is public domain in the US; the NIV (1978) is under active copyright held by Biblica. This distinction is the single most important rule in the sacred-texts subfolder. See [[sacred-texts/translation-rights-rule|translation-rights-rule]].

### 3. Political content — mostly legal but not platform-safe
Creating content about political figures or parties is generally legal (First Amendment protection for commentary and satire is broad). But:
- Living politicians have **right-of-publicity** interests that vary by state.
- Party logos and campaign slogans are often **trademarked**.
- Platform policy (Amazon Merch, Redbubble, Etsy, TeePublic, YouTube, Meta) restricts political content and can result in takedowns and account health dings regardless of legal merit.
See [[political-content/README|political-content]].

### 4. Comedian and comic quotes — mostly do not use
Jokes and comedy bits are **copyrightable creative expression**. Catchphrases are frequently **trademarked**. The persona of a living comedian is protected by **right of publicity**. The safe lane is narrow: historical wits who died before 1956 with works published before 1930. See [[brand-safety/comedian-quotes-warning|comedian-quotes-warning]] and [[12-Quotes/README|12-Quotes]].
